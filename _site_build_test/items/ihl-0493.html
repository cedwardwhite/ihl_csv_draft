Narrative Date:

Earliest Date of Creation or Publication:
1877
Description:
"This triptych was a product of two major Meiji artists, Kawanabe Kyōsai (1831-1889), who designed the scenic imagery, and Toyohara Kunichika, who focused on the characters. The women dressed in red hakama, a style of formal, pleated trousers, seen both in the left and center sheets, are engaged in the once traditional New Year’s custom of pulling young pines. Their garments and hairstyles evoke the imperial court of a millennium earlier when the lady-in-waiting known as Murasaki Shikibu wrote Tale of Genji. The elaborately attired gentleman (right) possibly portrays the novel’s central character, Prince Genji. But there is a twist: his unusual hairstyle appeared first in illustrations for the 1830s serialized parody, Rustic Genji, Fake Murasaki (偐紫田舎源氏) designed by Utagawa Kunisada, Kunichika’s teacher. To add further humorous time warps to the depiction, the British-designed Yamazato suspension bridge of 1870 spans the background."[1]
The artists have signed the print in the areas of their contribution. Kunichika’s signature in the right panel is proceeded with the two characters 人物 (“jinbutsu”) meaning “human figure by.”
Kunichika and Kyōsai were two of the most popular woodblock print artists of the 1870s. Contemporaries, they were “well-acquainted, but at times adversarial, companions.”[2] It was not unusual for publishers to have multiple artists work on different portions of a single print, each being assigned their design strength. Kyōsai and Kunichika first collaborated in 1875 on four single sheet prints in the series Collection of Famous Views of Modern Tokyo, published by the same publisher as this triptych. See this collection's print IHL.0688 for another collaborative print by these two artist.

[1] Didactics by Lynn Katsumoto for the exhibition "Ukiyo-e to Shin Hanga: Changing Tastes in Japanese Woodblock Prints" (November 20, 2021 - January 30, 2022)
[2] "Kawanabe Kyosai and Toyohara Kunichika," by Shigeru Oikawa appearing in Time Present and Time Past: Images of a Forgotten Master: Toyohara Kunichika (1835-1900), by Amy Reigle Newland, Hotei Publishing, 1999, p. 40, 42.
